Every Veterans Day, and every Memorial Day, the same text comes in.

Thank you for your service.

Ken has done this for twenty-five years. Without hesitation. He is a good friend. I know it is genuine. I know he cares. I smile when I get it. I value it.

I do not know how to respond other than, "Thanks, Ken."

That is the awkwardness. It is not that I am ungrateful. It is not that I want people to stop. I appreciate when someone means it. I just do not know what to do with being thanked for something I did not do for praise or recognition.

Sometimes, at a game, they ask the veterans to stand. Some of the other veterans consistently wear their veteran service gear and take that moment in. I am not one of those guys. If I stand, it is because my kids asked me to. They care about the service. I did not join so a stadium would notice.

## Why I joined.

I was in college. Decent grades. I had moved out of my parents' house and was trying to pay rent while working nearly full time, going to school full time, and trying to be a full-time college partyer as well. I was conflicted about a major. The usual story. Pre-med. Pre-law. Chemistry. History teacher. Whatever I had heard in class that week and decided would be the rest of my life.

I was floundering. Stuck in a small-town mindset in Western New York. I knew I was wasting my parents' money. I needed to get out and figure out who I was as a human being.

Over the winter holidays I went to visit my brother Tim at Schofield Barracks in Hawaii. I saw the camaraderie. I saw them marching together. I was drawn to it.

There was already a patriotic thread. The night Desert Storm started, I had my mom put an American flag on my Letterman jacket. My pride in being an American is not abstract. I put it on my jacket because I wanted to support our troops. I had read and understood how Vietnam veterans like my dad, the guys who actually came back from that war, did not receive that support. I wanted to make sure I supported our veterans and our active duty soldiers.

He was fortunate enough not to have been sent to the infantry in Vietnam. He went to cook school and then to Germany, which was rare for a draft that year. He was proud to be an Army cook, but he never wanted it for me. I was the youngest of three boys, and the one who was going to finish college. When I quit after my first year to enlist, he thought I was wasting an opportunity he never really had. Not that he wasn't proud. He thought I was walking away from something he did not get.

My grandpa Hill, my mom's dad, was proud to have served. Combat engineer in World War II. Battle of the Bulge. 291st Engineer Combat Battalion, in the Malmedy area of Belgium.

They helped stop Kampfgruppe Peiper, the armored spearhead of the 1st SS Panzer Division, by blowing bridges in its path. Joachim Peiper is the one the histories credit with slamming his fist and yelling about "the damned engineers." That phrase is in a book on my shelf. I can see it as I write this. There is a tremendous amount of pride in being the grandson of a veteran of one of our greatest generations. Grandpa Hill was also proud of having built the first bridge across the Rhine that let U.S. forces push into Germany.

My grandma Jeanette's father, my great-grandfather Fred Call, was a doughboy in World War I. Grandpa Hill's father-in-law. Same generation of service, same family line through her.

## What I want you to understand.

Being a veteran is about the brotherhood. My fellow brothers and sisters in arms. It is about being part of a team. The camaraderie of having served. Wearing the uniform. Giving yourself to something larger than yourself for the betterment of the nation.

It is also about who shows up in the same formation. Basic training was the melting pot. An immigrant from Guatemala trying to earn the right to become a citizen. Cajuns from the Louisiana bayou. Inner city kids from the streets of New Orleans. Guys from Texas. Boston. Kansas. Me, an Irish American kid from Western New York. The guy from Long Island who said Strong Island, who had flirted with gang violence in the streets of New York. A guy who had been part of the Rodney King riots in Compton. Live Oak, Florida. Butte, Montana. All of us figuring out how to work as a team.

That is the essence of America. What is possible when we come together. That is what I want a civilian to understand about being a veteran.

I served in Korea, at Camp Humphreys, on the side-looking radar of an old Mohawk. SCAM, if you know you know. I served next to KATUSAs, Korean augmentees to the U.S. Army, Korean military serving side by side with American soldiers. That camaraderie with the people of the Republic of Korea is part of what the service meant. I am fortunate never to have been part of a conflict. That does not mean I would not have done it. I recognize that my contribution is not the same as others.

My friend Z was in the New York Army National Guard. He was at Ground Zero the day after the towers fell. He did three deployments. He carried the exposure and what he witnessed on those tours. He ultimately lost his battle with those demons.

I am grateful for those who have served. For those who have sacrificed. For the families who paid the ultimate price when someone they loved gave their life in service and defense of this country. For seven years I worked with Teresa Garcia, a Gold Star mother who lost her son Antonio in an Army training accident. The pride she carries as a Gold Star mother is real. It does not replace the ultimate sacrifice her family made. She faces that every day.

To my family. My great-grandfather, my grandfather, my grandpa Hill, Uncle Jack, Bob Miller, and Mike Miller. Thank you for your service. Rest in peace. And to my dad, my uncle Mike, my brother Tim, and my nephew Matt. Thank you for your service.

To all the boys I served with. Harley, Heri, Kelly, Ferg, Parks, Mixon, the Swede, Westmoreland, SGT Adams, SSG Foulkes, Rene, Rick, Z, Corbett, and so many more. Love you all. Miss you guys.

Ken will text me again. I will write back, "Thanks, Ken." I mean it. The recognition is not why any of us put the uniform on. The team is. The country is. The chance to become someone larger than the kid who could not pick a major.

Jim
